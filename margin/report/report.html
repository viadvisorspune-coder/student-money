01 / Research at a glance

The question changed from tracking money to understanding decisions.

The project did not begin by asking how to make students budget better.

Earlier research showed that student spending is shaped by social situations, future commitments, convenience, comfort, perceived value, mental allocation and the effort required to calculate financial consequences.

This shifted the testing question:

Can making financial consequences visible at the moment of spending make spending decisions more conscious?

Testing question

The target outcome was not simply reduced spending.

A participant could still make the purchase and demonstrate conscious consideration.

01 / The behavioural gap

The information already exists. The problem is when it becomes available.

A student may already know:

  • how much money they have;
  • how much they have spent;
  • that a future expense is coming;
  • roughly how much they can afford.

But that information does not automatically enter the active decision.

What exactly were we testing?

02 / Target behaviour

We were testing a micro-behaviour, not an app.

Target behaviour

Before or during a discretionary spending decision, the participant notices relevant financial context, understands the likely consequence of the proposed expense, and uses that information as one input into the decision.

04 / What the baseline changed

Awareness did not come from a better chart. It came from seeing accumulation.

The baseline suggested that a single small expense was not necessarily the meaningful unit.

The participant already knew they bought snacks. What changed was the visibility of how often.

This matters because the prototype originally contained considerable historical information. The baseline suggested that simply displaying more historical data was unlikely to be enough.

The meaningful unit could be

Frequency × repetition × accumulation

05 / D1, behavioural analysis

D1 did not reject the concept. D1 rejected the distance between the concept and the decision.

D1 first explored the app as a financial record: monthly spending, categories, patterns and plans.

But when asked to imagine actually spending, D1 asked:

“How would this help me exactly?”

This is the critical break. Later in the session, the “spending something” interaction became the strongest part of the prototype.

The issue was not

“Can D1 understand the numbers?”

It was

“Why would I open this now?”

06 / Finding 03, past spending and future consequence

Historical information explains behaviour. Consequence information can support a decision.

The prototype originally gave substantial space to monthly spending, categories, patterns and transaction history. But the strongest direct response came around potential future spending.

D1  called the potential-spending feature a highlight.

D2  repeatedly connected value to upcoming expenses.

D3  asked to see how a proposed expense affects other spending.

06 / Finding 04, social context

Financial reasoning competes with other things that matter.

The spending decision is rarely purely financial. Participants describe wanting to stay with friends, being hungry, avoiding bad food, comfort, responsibility toward another person, convenience and perceived value.

C1  behaviour is strongly socially cued.

D1  sometimes prioritises comfort or responsibility.

C5  shows that visible calculation can itself feel socially awkward.

06 / Finding 05, awareness does not always mean spend less

The decision can remain the same and still become more conscious.

A participant may see “this purchase leaves me with less money” and still decide “I want it enough to spend that money.” That is not necessarily a failed intervention.

The intended behaviour is know, consider, choose; not know, refuse.

Key statement

Financial consciousness is not the same thing as financial restriction.

06 / Finding 06, mental accounting

Saving money in one context can create permission to spend in another.

D2's contextual record is useful here. D2 planned to spend within a certain range on one outing. When D2 spent less than expected, that did not necessarily remain as savings. The perceived surplus later loosened spending elsewhere.

Insight

Showing “under budget” is not automatically behaviourally positive. The relevant question is: what does the person believe the remaining money means?

1Planned amount
2Spend less than expected
3Perceived slack
4More spending elsewhere
ContradictionAwareness can reduce spontaneous spending (B1)
versus
 Awareness can create perceived spending slack (D2)
EvidenceD2's contextual record of surplus loosening later spending. Observed
InterpretationRemaining money is read as permission, not as a buffer.
Design implicationAvoid “under budget” praise; show remaining money against what is coming.
Remaining unknownHow common this reading is beyond one record.

08 / Break, interpretation, design decision

Not every problem became a feature.

Observed break Observed

Use case unclear, and consequence difficult to access

Interpretation

The intervention is too detached from the actual decision.

Design decision

Move the relevant financial consequence closer to the spending moment.

Prediction Unknown

The participant should require fewer steps to understand the consequence.

Why it matters
Each design change in V2 traces back to an observed break, not to a feature request.

Not taken forward
Requests that did not address the break, such as more categories or more charts, were not added.
