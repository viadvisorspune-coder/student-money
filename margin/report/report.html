01 / Research at a glance

The question changed from tracking money to understanding decisions.

The project did not begin by asking how to make students budget better.

Earlier research showed that student spending is shaped by social situations, future commitments, convenience, comfort, perceived value, mental allocation and the effort required to calculate financial consequences. No single one of these explained how students spent; each appeared in a different form for different people.

Those findings made tracking look like the wrong unit of design. A record of past spending describes what happened. It does not reach the moment when a student is deciding whether to spend. The research question therefore moved from how students record money to how, and when, financial information enters a decision.

Can making financial consequences visible at the moment of spending make spending decisions more conscious?

Testing question

The target outcome was not simply reduced spending. A participant could still make the purchase and demonstrate conscious consideration.

For that reason this report treats consideration, not a lower total, as the outcome of interest. Each session is read for evidence that financial context was noticed, understood and weighed before a choice was made.

How the question moved
  • Tracking
  • Visibility
  • Awareness
  • Consideration
  • Conscious decision
  • Reflection

Consideration is the stage this report is about.

How to read this report

Every claim is labelled observed, stated, inferred or unknown (page 08). Participants are shown by code. Figures appear only where source records support them; illustrative figures are marked.

01 / The behavioural gap

The information already exists. The problem is when it becomes available.

A student may already know:

  • how much money they have;
  • how much they have spent;
  • that a future expense is coming;
  • roughly how much they can afford.

But that information does not automatically enter the active decision.

C4's earlier research record is particularly important here. C4 knew about a Friday dinner, but when the Wednesday food decision occurred, the immediate situation became more salient. Nothing was missing from what C4 knew. What was missing was the dinner's presence in the decision.

Two kinds of availability

Information is technically available when it is stored somewhere the student could look it up: a balance in a banking app, a list of transactions, a plan in a calendar or a budget in a note.

Information is mentally available when it is present in the student's thinking at the moment of choosing. In a live decision, that space is taken by immediate questions: what do I want, what are my friends doing, am I hungry, is this worth it.

Margin does not add new information. It tests whether moving existing information across this gap changes what a student considers.

02 / Research question

What exactly were we testing?

Can making financial consequences visible at the moment of spending make spending decisions more conscious?

Primary question

Not: “Will students spend less?”

The test was concerned with whether financial information enters the reasoning process before a decision is completed.

A spend-less measure would have misread the question in both directions. It would count a participant who knowingly chose to buy as a failure, and a participant who skipped a purchase for unrelated reasons as a success. It would also push the product toward restriction, which D2 explicitly did not want (page 17).

What counts as a conscious decision

A conscious decision may result in any of these outcomes:

BuyBuy lessDelayChoose something elseDo not buyBuy, knowing the consequence

What makes each one conscious is the same: before committing, the participant can say what the purchase does to their money. The outcome itself is left to them.

This distinction runs through the rest of the report. Where a finding says a decision “stayed the same,” that is not read as a failure of the intervention (page 26).

Outcome of interest

Consideration before commitment

Not the outcome

Total spent, money saved, or number of purchases avoided.

02 / Target behaviour

We were testing a micro-behaviour, not an app.

Target behaviour

Before or during a discretionary spending decision, the participant notices relevant financial context, understands the likely consequence of the proposed expense, and uses that information as one input into the decision.

Testing an app as a whole invites questions about whether people like it. Testing a micro-behaviour asks something narrower and more useful: at a specific moment, did a specific thing happen? The target behaviour above breaks into five steps, each with a signal that can be seen or heard in a session.

What would this look like in behaviour?

StepObservable test signal
1NoticeLooks at financial information
2UnderstandAccurately explains it
3ConnectRelates it to the purchase
4ConsiderArticulates the consequence or trade-off
5DecideMaintains, changes, delays or substitutes the purchase

The ladder is the analytical framework for the participant pages that follow. Each session is read step by step. Where a participant's reasoning stops before the next step, that point is treated as the behavioural break, and the break, not the participant's overall opinion, is what the design responds to.

Step 4 is the target

A decision at step 5 without step 4 is a choice, but not a conscious one in the sense used here.

02 / What counts as evidence

Three things had to stay separate.

Most prototype evidence in this project comes from people reacting to hypothetical spending situations. That material mixes what people did, what they say they would do and what the research team reads into both. Kept together, a sentence such as “I might end up using it” can drift into a claim about adoption.

Every claim in this report therefore carries one of four labels. The worked example below is taken from the D1 V1 prototype session.

Rule

A stated preference is never reported as behaviour, and an inference is never reported as a finding without its label.

Observed

What the participant actually did or said.

D1 questioned the purpose of the feature and later identified “spending something” as the key interaction.

Stated

What the participant said they would prefer or do.

D1 said they would be more inclined to use the product if transactions were automatic.

Inferred

What the research team believes the behaviour may mean.

Reducing transaction-entry effort may be a condition for adoption, rather than the intervention itself.

Unknown

What the current evidence cannot answer.

Whether automatic capture would lead D1 to use the product in a real spending moment.

The same labels recur on every participant and finding page. Two further marks are used: Source record needed where a claim depends on material not yet in the archive, and Illustrative where a figure is an example rather than data.

03 / Testing architecture

The test was structured around the decision, not the interface.

Each session followed the same sequence. The participant was not asked to explore the app and give feedback; they were placed in a spending situation, shown financial context, and asked what they would do. The interface was the means of showing context, not the object of the test.

StepWhat happensWhat it produces
01 ObserveSix-day natural spending reflectionA baseline of what visibility alone does (B1)
02 EncounterParticipant is placed in a hypothetical spending situationA decision to reason about
03 ReflectPrototype introduces financial contextReactions to the information

05 AnalyseIdentify the behavioural breakThe ladder step where reasoning stopped

The six-day phase is a behavioural probe, not proof that the prototype caused later behaviour change. It sits before the prototype so that the effect of simply paying attention to spending can be seen on its own.

Steps 02 to 05 are documented for the direct sessions. A clean second round with fresh participants is not yet documented; page 36 sets out what that means for the claims.

Faculty method

The method requires an observable micro-behaviour, a fixed success criterion, one change between comparable rounds and fresh participants for Round 2.

04 / The six-day baseline

Before the prototype: what happened when spending simply became visible?

Six days of self-observation, record B1

The purpose of the six-day exercise was not to prove product impact. It was to see what happened when spending became more salient through observation and reflection alone, with no prototype involved.

One source-backed self-observation record describes a change in attention toward small, frequent purchases. The participant became more aware of repeated snack purchases and avoided some snacks and autos after seeing the accumulation.

The sequence the record describes is simple: a small purchase repeats across days; the repetition becomes visible; awareness changes. No chart or tool was involved. The change came from attention.

This finding comes with a caution. Reflecting on spending for six days may itself increase financial salience, so the diary is part of the effect it records (page 43).

04 / What the baseline changed

Awareness did not come from a better chart. It came from seeing accumulation.

The baseline suggested that a single small expense was not necessarily the meaningful unit.

The meaningful unit could be

Frequency × repetition × accumulation

The participant already knew they bought snacks. What changed was the visibility of how often. A single ₹60 purchase does not register as a decision worth weighing; the same purchase seen for the fourth time in a week reads differently.

This matters because the prototype originally contained considerable historical information: monthly totals, categories and patterns. The baseline suggested that simply displaying more historical data was unlikely to be enough. What made the difference in B1 was not the quantity of information but the moment it became noticeable, which points toward the timing question the rest of the testing takes up.

04 / The unit of analysis

A purchase is not just a transaction. It is a decision episode.

A transaction record shows an amount, a time and a merchant. It says nothing about the situation that produced it. Two identical ₹300 dinners can come from very different decisions: one planned for a week, the other made in thirty seconds because friends were already ordering. Treating them as equivalent hides exactly what this project needed to see.

The report therefore analyses each purchase as a decision episode with six parts.

PartWhat it contains
ContextFriend invitation, hunger, bad mess food, a future plan, time pressure
Immediate desire or needWhat the person wants now
Financial realityCurrent money, past spending, future commitment
Mental trade-offWeighing the want against the financial reality
DecisionThe choice made
Post-purchase reflectionHow the choice is judged afterwards

This model allows the research to analyse why a purchase happened rather than treating every transaction as equivalent. It also locates the intervention. Margin is meant to act between financial reality and the mental trade-off: the last point at which information can still change the decision.

06 / Finding 05, awareness does not always mean spend less

The decision can remain the same and still become more conscious.

A participant may see “this purchase leaves me with less money” and still decide “I want it enough to spend that money.” That is not necessarily a failed intervention.

The intended behaviour is know, consider, choose; not know, refuse.

Key statement

Financial consciousness is not the same thing as financial restriction.

06 / Finding 06, mental accounting

Saving money in one context can create permission to spend in another.

D2's contextual record is useful here. D2 planned to spend within a certain range on one outing. When D2 spent less than expected, that did not necessarily remain as savings. The perceived surplus later loosened spending elsewhere.
